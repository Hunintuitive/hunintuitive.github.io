# Choosing an Air Conditioner Wisely - Technical Requirements Before Price

*How do you choose an air conditioner that performs well not only on paper, but in the conditions in which you will actually use it?*

## 📝 TL;DR

Choosing an air conditioner should not start with a brand, a price list or a collection of attractive features. It should start with understanding the building, its heating and cooling requirements, the conditions in which the unit will operate, and the level of comfort expected.

Only then should we compare specific models, verify their specifications and consider the available budget.

The goal is not to find the most expensive unit or the one with the longest feature list. It is to get the technical performance we genuinely need for our money.

---

## 🎯 1. Define the task before choosing the equipment

An inverter split air conditioner is more than a cooling appliance with a fan. It is an air-to-air heat pump that can provide both cooling and heating.

In heating mode, it does not simply convert all the electricity it consumes directly into heat. Instead, it transfers heat from the outdoor environment into the building. This is why it can deliver more heat energy than the electrical energy it consumes.

That does not mean its efficiency remains constant under all conditions. Actual performance depends on outdoor temperature, humidity, operating load, defrost cycles and the building's heat demand.

So the first questions should not be about brands. They should be:

- How many rooms and how much floor area need heating or cooling?
- How well insulated is the building?
- What are the heat gains and heat losses of each room?
- At what outdoor temperatures should the system provide heating?
- Will it operate throughout the day or only at certain times?
- What indoor temperature, airflow and noise level are acceptable?

Floor area alone is not enough to size a system. Two apartments of identical size can have very different heating requirements.

Room volume is another useful way to describe the space. It can be calculated using this simple formula:

**V = A × h**

where:
- **V** is the room volume in cubic metres (m³);
- **A** is the floor area in square metres (m²);
- **h** is the ceiling height in metres (m).

For example, a room with a floor area of 20 m² and a ceiling height of 2.7 metres has a volume of:

**20 m² × 2.7 m = 54 m³**

Room volume describes the space more completely than floor area alone, and it can also be useful when assessing air exchange and ventilation-related heat loads. However, it is not sufficient on its own to determine the required air-conditioner capacity. A universal W/m³ or W/m² rule of thumb should not be used as a substitute for proper sizing. The actual cooling heat load and heating heat loss need to be assessed separately for each room.

This assessment should take into account, among other things:

- the insulation of external walls, ceilings and floors;
- the size, type and orientation of windows;
- solar exposure and shading;
- the difference between outdoor and desired indoor temperatures;
- air exchange, ventilation and possible air leakage;
- heat generated by occupants, lighting and electrical equipment;
- adjacent rooms and actual usage patterns.

Heating heat loss and cooling heat gain are not necessarily the same. For example, a large south-facing glazed area may create a substantial cooling load in summer, while its winter heat loss depends on the glazing and insulation characteristics. Room volume is therefore useful descriptive information, but sizing must account for the combined effects of heat transfer through the building envelope, solar gains, internal loads and air exchange.

Room volume is therefore one useful input to the sizing process, not a sizing method in itself. Final capacity selection should be based on room-by-room heating and cooling load calculations.

## 📊 2. What do the efficiency figures actually tell us?

### COP - instantaneous heating efficiency

COP, or Coefficient of Performance, indicates how much heat a heat pump delivers for each unit of electricity it consumes under specified test conditions.

A COP of 4 means that 1 kWh of electricity delivers 4 kWh of heat.

The additional heat is not created from nothing: it is transferred from the surrounding environment.

COP is not a fixed value. Performance at +7 °C can be very different from performance at -10 °C. Humidity and defrost operation also affect real-world performance.

### SCOP - seasonal heating efficiency

SCOP is a standardised seasonal heating efficiency indicator. It is useful for comparing products, but it is not a guarantee of the seasonal efficiency that a particular unit will achieve in a particular home.

### SEER - seasonal cooling efficiency

SEER describes seasonal energy efficiency in cooling mode.

SCOP and SEER are useful starting points, but they do not replace a more detailed technical assessment. The European EPREL database provides additional information, including heating performance for different climates, design loads, sound power and refrigerant type.

**A high SCOP figure alone does not prove that a unit will be the best choice for your home.**

## ⚙️ 3. Performance Is About More Than a Single Number

Air conditioner specifications often place the nominal cooling and heating capacities in the most prominent position. These are important figures, but they are not enough on their own. When choosing the right unit, we also need to consider its operating capacity range, modulation capability, part-load efficiency and actual heating output at low outdoor temperatures.

### Minimum and maximum output

With inverter equipment, it is particularly important to know how low the unit can reduce its output and what maximum capacity it can provide.

If a unit is oversized for the room's heating or cooling demand and cannot reduce its output sufficiently, it may cycle on and off more frequently. This can have a negative impact on comfort and operating efficiency.

Correct sizing therefore does not simply mean choosing a larger unit to be on the safe side. Oversizing can have disadvantages too.

### Balancing Capacity Reserve and Modulation Capability

When sizing an air conditioner, it is important not only to avoid oversizing, but also to make sure the unit is not undersized.

Ideally, the selected unit should have sufficient capacity to meet the building's heating or cooling demand without constantly operating at its maximum output. During extremely cold winter days or particularly hot summer weather, it is important to have enough reserve capacity to maintain the required indoor conditions.

For this reason, selecting a unit with some additional capacity can be a sensible approach. However, the objective is not to oversize the system excessively, but to find the right balance.

An oversized unit may struggle to reduce its output sufficiently during milder weather, when the room's heating or cooling demand is relatively low. If its minimum output is still higher than the actual demand, the unit may cycle on and off more frequently, potentially reducing comfort and operating efficiency.

For example, a 5 kW air conditioner is not necessarily a better choice for a room where a properly selected 3.5 kW unit can already provide sufficient capacity. The larger model may offer an advantage under extreme conditions, but this advantage can be offset if its minimum output is too high for the room's actual requirements.

It is also important to remember that nominal capacity alone does not tell the whole story. Two air conditioners with the same nominal capacity can differ considerably in their actual heating output at low outdoor temperatures and in their ability to modulate down to lower output levels.

### Same Nominal Capacity, Different Technical Design

Two air conditioners with the same nominal capacity can differ significantly in terms of engineering quality, technical design and actual performance.

For example, two 3.5 kW inverter air conditioners do not necessarily provide the same heating capacity, efficiency or modulation capability. The difference may lie not in their nominal capacity, but in the technology and engineering behind it.

Higher-performance models may offer advantages in several areas, including:

- larger or more efficient heat exchangers;
- more advanced compressor technology with a wider modulation range;
- an optimised refrigerant circuit and refrigerant flow control;
- more precise electronic control systems;
- better part-load performance;
- more effective defrost strategies.

Taken together, these features can help a unit achieve higher efficiency at the same nominal capacity, maintain greater heating output at lower outdoor temperatures or operate efficiently across a wider modulation range.

This is why comparing air conditioners based solely on their nominal kW rating or SCOP figure is not sufficient. It is also worth examining part-load efficiency, available heating capacity at different outdoor temperatures, minimum and maximum operating output, and the technical design of the system.

A larger heat exchanger, a more advanced compressor or an optimised refrigerant circuit does not automatically guarantee better efficiency. These features can provide technical advantages, but their actual benefits must be demonstrated by the performance of the complete system and supported by measurement data.

**The key point is that we do not necessarily need a larger air conditioner. In some cases, we need a better-engineered, more efficient unit that can deliver the same nominal capacity with lower energy consumption.**

### Mono-split or multi-split?

When several rooms need air conditioning, the decision is not only how many indoor units are required, but also whether to use separate mono-split systems or a multi-split system connected to a shared outdoor unit.

Multi-split systems have genuine advantages. One outdoor unit can serve several indoor units, reducing the number of outdoor units and the space they occupy. This can be particularly useful where façade rules, building regulations or aesthetic considerations limit the number of outdoor units that can be installed.

However, a multi-split is not simply several mono-split systems combined into one. The outdoor unit has a finite capacity, which is shared across the connected indoor units according to the system's design and operating conditions. For example, connecting two indoor units rated at 3.5 kW each to a 5 kW outdoor unit does not mean that both can deliver 3.5 kW simultaneously. The actual combined output depends on the specific combination, outdoor temperature, operating conditions and the manufacturer's combination tables. The sum of the indoor units' nominal ratings is therefore not the same as the system's simultaneous capacity.

Part-load operation also deserves attention. If only one indoor unit is running, the outdoor unit's minimum capacity may, in some systems, be higher than the room's current demand. This can lead to more frequent cycling. That is not true of every multi-split system: it must be checked against the specific model's technical data, minimum output and manufacturer combination tables.

Conventional residential multi-split systems also usually operate in a common mode: the connected indoor units generally heat or cool, rather than allowing one room to be heated while another is cooled. Specialised VRF systems with heat recovery can be exceptions, so the documentation for the specific system should always be checked.

The pipework serving several indoor units can be more complex, with additional connections and limits on pipe length and height differences. Accurate design, installation and commissioning are therefore particularly important. The shared outdoor unit is also a common point of failure: certain faults can affect every indoor unit connected to it.

Two or more separate mono-split systems, by contrast, have independent outdoor units and compressors, allow independent control, and provide greater operational independence. If one system fails, the other may continue to operate. Part-load flexibility may also be better, depending on the specific units. The disadvantages are having more outdoor units, needing more space and, in some cases, paying more for the initial installation.

There is no universally superior option: multi-split and separate mono-split systems involve different trade-offs. The decision should be based on the manufacturer's capacity data for the exact indoor/outdoor combinations, part-load behaviour, installation constraints and the consequences of a possible failure.

### Heating capacity at low outdoor temperatures

We need to know how much heat the unit can actually deliver at different outdoor temperatures.

Look for manufacturer capacity data at conditions such as:

- +7 °C;
- +2 °C;
- -7 °C;
- -10 °C;
- -15 °C;
- ideally -20 °C.

A statement that a unit can “heat down to -20 °C” is not enough. It may only describe the permitted operating range. It does not tell us how much heating capacity or efficiency remains at that temperature.

**The minimum operating temperature is not the same as the available heating capacity at that temperature.**

## ❄️ 4. Defrosting: a critical issue that is often overlooked

Under certain conditions, frost or ice can form on the outdoor heat exchanger of an air-source heat pump. The system must periodically remove it.

This can be particularly relevant in damp, foggy weather around freezing temperatures. Outdoor humidity and the temperature of the heat exchanger can combine to create favourable conditions for frost formation.

Defrosting is a normal operating process, not a fault in itself. The important questions are:

- Under what conditions does defrosting begin?
- How frequently does it occur?
- How long does each cycle take?
- How much does it interrupt indoor heat delivery?
- How quickly does normal heating resume?
- Is the water produced during defrosting drained properly?
- Does the outdoor unit's design, including any base-pan heater, help prevent ice accumulation?

The defrost strategy affects both the overall system efficiency and indoor comfort. Therefore, it is not enough to simply check whether the unit is equipped with a base pan heater and a compressor crankcase heater, or the lowest outdoor temperature at which operation is permitted. The mere presence of these components does not necessarily indicate superior heating performance. What matters far more is the effectiveness of the defrost control strategy, the conditions under which defrost cycles are initiated and carried out, and how quickly the unit resumes normal heating operation afterwards.

Independent measurements and detailed technical documentation provide a more reliable picture of actual behaviour.

## 🌬️ 5. Airflow, noise and comfort

A unit can look efficient on paper and still be unpleasant to use every day.

Consider:

- Airflow at different fan speeds.
- How far and in which direction the air can be delivered.
- How precisely the airflow direction can be controlled.
- Noise at low and high fan speeds.
- Temperature distribution throughout the room.

Placement can be just as important as nominal capacity. A poorly positioned indoor unit may create uncomfortable drafts while delivering little warm air to other parts of the home.

## 🔧 6. Maintenance and repairability

An air conditioner matters long after it has been purchased and installed. It will need cleaning, maintenance and possibly repairs over many years.

Check:

- How easily can the indoor heat exchanger be cleaned?
- Is the cross-flow fan accessible?
- Can the condensate tray be cleaned properly?
- How much disassembly is required for thorough cleaning?
- Are important spare parts available?
- Is there a reliable local service network?
- What are the warranty conditions and maintenance obligations?

Self-cleaning functions can be useful, but they do not replace physical cleaning and professional maintenance.

A unit that is easy to clean and repair may be a better long-term choice than one with impressive features but poor serviceability.

## ⚡ 7. Electrical installation and Hungary's H tariff

The electrical installation should not be treated as an afterthought.

Check:

- available phases and connection capacity;
- the main circuit breaker;
- the load on the relevant circuits;
- cable cross-sections and condition;
- required electrical protection;
- the selected unit's maximum electrical input and current.

A qualified electrician should assess the installation. Nominal heating or cooling capacity alone cannot establish whether the existing electrical system can safely support new equipment.

In Hungary, the H tariff is available for eligible heat-pump equipment connected to a separately metered circuit and meeting the applicable requirements. Current MVM guidance requires air-to-air heat pumps to have a seasonal performance factor (SCOP) of at least 3.4; the equipment must also be permanently connected to the separately metered circuit rather than plugged in. The discounted heating period runs from 15 October to 15 April. Before purchasing, check the exact unit's eligibility and connection requirements with the relevant distribution network operator, as the specific technical and administrative conditions matter.

The H tariff can be a significant advantage, but it is not the only factor in operating costs. Actual efficiency, the building's heat demand, usage patterns, installation costs and maintenance all matter.

## 🏠 8. An illustrative apartment

Consider an apartment with a **nominal floor area of 60 m²**, comprising two rooms, a kitchen-dining area, a bathroom, a separate WC, a pantry and an entrance hall.

The stated floor areas of the individual rooms are:

| Room | Floor area |
|---|---:|
| Room 1 | 21.26 m² |
| Room 2 | 16.50 m² |
| Kitchen-dining area | 11.93 m² |
| Bathroom | 3.40 m² |
| WC | 0.97 m² |
| Pantry | 0.98 m² |
| Entrance hall | 5.19 m² |
| **Total of the listed rooms** | **60.23 m²** |

The apartment's stated nominal floor area is 60 m², while the sum of the listed room areas is 60.23 m². We will not arbitrarily adjust the 0.23 m² difference; the two figures should be treated separately.

Each of the two rooms has a Gree Amber GWH12YC-K6DNA1A/I indoor unit, purchased around 2018-2019.

The model's nominal cooling capacity is 3.5 kW and its nominal heating capacity is 3.81 kW. The energy label lists a SCOP of 4.4 for the average climate and a SEER of 8.5 for cooling. These are standardised manufacturer product figures, not measurements taken in this apartment, and they do not mean that the unit delivers its nominal heating capacity at every outdoor temperature.

There is no separate air conditioning unit in the kitchen and dining area. When needed, a Midea dehumidifier with a stated moisture extraction capacity of 50 litres per day is used. This rating applies under specified warm and humid test conditions; actual extraction can be much lower in a cooler or drier room. During operation, the unit also releases heat, which can contribute to warming the room. The stated 1,060 W figure should be treated as maximum electrical input, not as continuous nominal consumption.

### What does the long-term experience tell us?

The two air conditioners have been sufficient to heat the apartment. During the day, the indoor temperature is generally kept at 23-24 °C. Both units are switched off overnight.

During very cold weather, the indoor temperature may fall to around 18 °C by morning. This is still comfortable for sleeping for the occupants. There have also been nights when the outdoor temperature dropped to around -10 to -15 °C, yet the system has continued to meet the apartment's heating needs since the units were purchased around 2018-2019.

This is valuable real-world experience, but it is not a laboratory measurement. It does not tell us the exact heating capacity or COP of the units at a particular outdoor temperature. It does, however, illustrate how building heat retention, equipment placement and usage patterns work together to determine whether a system is suitable.

**The lesson is not that every 60 m² apartment needs two 3.5 kW air conditioners.** The lesson is that sizing must be based on the specific building and the way it is actually used.

## 💧 9. A dehumidifier as a supplementary appliance

A dehumidifier can be a particularly useful addition to a heating system during the winter months. Everyday activities such as cooking in the kitchen, showering in the bathroom and drying clothes indoors can release significant amounts of moisture into the air. During cold weather, condensation is more likely to form near external walls and other colder surfaces. Under suitable conditions, this can also increase the risk of mould growth.

A compressor-based, heat pump dehumidifier - such as the Midea unit used in this example - performs two functions at the same time: it reduces the moisture content of the air while also releasing heat into the room during operation.

Through its refrigeration circuit, the unit releases into the room the latent heat liberated when water vapour extracted from the air condenses. The electrical energy consumed by the appliance also ultimately appears as heat. The heat delivered to the room therefore comes from both the condensation process and the electricity used by the unit. This is not “free” heating: energy was also required when the moisture originally evaporated, and the dehumidifier's primary purpose is moisture removal, not economical space heating.

**If dehumidification is already necessary, the heat released by the appliance can therefore be a useful side effect during the winter months.** This does not automatically mean that a dehumidifier is a more economical heating option than an electric resistance heater in every situation. The actual outcome depends on the operating conditions, energy consumption and whether dehumidification is needed in the first place.

However, it is important to note that a dehumidifier is not a substitute for adequate heating, proper ventilation or addressing the underlying sources of moisture. When assessing the overall energy efficiency and cost-effectiveness of the system, the energy required for dehumidification and the unit's actual performance must also be taken into account.

The Midea unit used in this example has a stated moisture extraction capacity of 50 litres per day, measured by manufacturers under specified temperature and humidity conditions. This should not be interpreted as the expected daily output in a cold winter apartment: extraction can fall substantially at lower temperatures and relative humidity. The 1,060 W figure is the maximum electrical input, not necessarily the unit's continuous or typical operating consumption. For example, the Midea MDDP-50DEN7-QA3-WIFI data sheet specifies the 50 litres/day extraction rate at test conditions of 30°C and 80% relative humidity; under those same conditions, power input is 850 W, while 1,060 W is the maximum input. This applies to the example only if it is the exact model variant being used. Always check the nameplate and technical data sheet for the actual unit.

## 🔍 10. How to investigate specific models

Once the technical requirements have been established, it is time to examine the products available on the market.

Useful sources include:

- manufacturer data sheets;
- service manuals and performance charts;
- the European EPREL database;
- independent product tests and measurements;
- technical videos and explanatory material;
- long-term user experiences;
- feedback from local installers and service technicians.

Useful starting points for verification:

- [European Commission – air-conditioner energy labels and EPREL data](https://energy-efficient-products.ec.europa.eu/product-list/air-conditioners-and-comfort-fans_en)
- [MVM Network – H tariff declaration and technical conditions](https://mvmhalozat.hu/attachments/40414)
- [Natural Resources Canada – heat pumps and defrost cycles](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/energy-star/heating-cooling-heat-pump)
- [Midea – MDDP dehumidifier product specifications](https://www.midea.com.ua/uk/products/pobutovi-kondytsionery-osushuvachi-povitrya/osushuvachi-povitrya/mddp)

It is important to distinguish between different kinds of information:

| Information type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Manufacturer specification | Technical data published by the manufacturer |
| Independent measurement | Performance measured by an external party |
| User experience | An account based on real-world use |
| Inference | An interpretation drawn from multiple pieces of information |
| Unknown | A question for which sufficient evidence is not available |

A marketing claim is not necessarily false, but it is not independent evidence.

## 💰 11. When should price enter the decision?

Only after the technical requirements have been established.

It is useful to compare three categories:

**A. Technical benchmark**

The models offering the strongest technical performance, regardless of price. They show what is available on the market.

**B. Best value for money**

Units that meet the important technical requirements without necessarily being the most expensive.

**C. Cost-optimised choice**

Less expensive models, with their compromises clearly identified.

The total cost includes more than the purchase price:

- installation;
- possible electrical upgrades;
- energy consumption;
- maintenance;
- repairs;
- spare-part availability.

This is the total cost of ownership (TCO).

# 🤖 How to use AI when choosing an air conditioner

The prompt below is designed to prevent the AI from immediately recommending brands and models. It should first assess the property and the way the equipment will be used, then establish the technical requirements.

Only after that should it compare specific products and consider the budget.

## Copy-and-use prompt

**Role and objective**

Act as a thorough and critical technical adviser helping me choose an inverter split air conditioner for both heating and cooling.

Do not recommend products immediately. First assess the property, usage requirements and technical conditions. Then establish the ideal technical specifications. Only after that should you consider specific brands, models and prices.

**1. Assess the requirements**

- the property's total floor area, ceiling height and floor plan;
- the floor area and ceiling height of every room, and calculate each room's volume in cubic metres (m³);
- room layout, connections and possibilities for air to circulate between rooms;
- floor level, orientation, roof or heated neighbours above;
- external walls, ceilings, floors, insulation, windows and shading;
- existing heating and cooling systems;
- desired daytime and nighttime temperatures;
- daily usage patterns;
- noise, airflow and comfort requirements;
- the electrical installation, phases, circuit breakers and any special electricity tariff.

Ask one important question at a time whenever possible. If I do not know an answer, help me understand how to find it.

**2. Establish the technical requirements**

Based on the assessment, determine the following separately for each room:

- required heating and cooling capacity;
- minimum and maximum output;
- suitable modulation range;
- required SCOP and SEER values;
- heating capacity at low outdoor temperatures;
- defrost performance requirements;
- airflow, noise and air-direction requirements;
- maintenance and repairability requirements;
- electrical installation requirements.

Calculate each room's volume (floor area × ceiling height) and use it as one of the sizing inputs, but do not use it on its own to determine the required capacity. Do not apply a universal W/m³ or W/m² rule of thumb for final sizing. Assess heating heat loss and cooling heat gain separately, taking into account the insulation of external walls, ceilings and floors; window size, type and orientation; solar exposure and shading; air exchange, ventilation and air leakage; internal heat gains; adjacent rooms; and usage patterns.

If several rooms need air conditioning, compare a multi-split system with two or more separate mono-split systems. For multi-split systems, check the manufacturer's combination tables for the exact configuration, simultaneous heating/cooling capacity, outdoor-unit capacity, minimum system output and operation when only one indoor unit is running. Also assess part-load operation, controllability, failure risks, installation and maintenance requirements.

Do not select equipment based solely on floor area or room volume. Do not estimate capacity from missing data or infer minimum/maximum output figures that the manufacturer has not published. If an important specification is unavailable, label it as unknown. If there is not enough information, state what is missing and what additional on-site assessment or heating/cooling load calculation is required.

If the Hungarian H tariff is relevant, verify the current eligibility conditions as well. Current MVM guidance specifies a minimum SCOP of 3.4 for air-to-air heat pumps, but the eligibility of the exact model and its electrical connection must be confirmed with the relevant distribution network operator.

**3. Evidence and sources**

When investigating specific models, prioritise:

- manufacturer data sheets;
- service manuals;
- performance charts;
- EPREL data;
- independent tests;
- real-world measurements;
- long-term user experiences.

For every important claim, distinguish between:

1. verified technical data;
2. manufacturer claims;
3. independent measurements;
4. user experiences;
5. conclusions or inferences;
6. unknown or unverifiable information.

Do not treat marketing claims as independent evidence. If a specification cannot be found, say so rather than filling the gap with assumptions.

**4. Compare specific models**

Once the technical requirements have been established, investigate at least three categories:

- technical benchmark;
- best value for money;
- cost-optimised option.

For each model, explain its advantages, disadvantages, uncertainties and compromises.

**5. Cost and operating economics**

Only consider the budget after the technical assessment.

Where possible, include:

- equipment price;
- installation;
- electrical upgrades;
- estimated annual electricity consumption;
- electricity tariffs;
- maintenance;
- repairs and spare-part availability.

Keep initial investment and total lifetime cost separate.

**6. Critical review**

Before making a final recommendation, check:

- Is the unit oversized?
- Is its minimum output sufficiently low?
- Does it provide adequate heating capacity in cold weather?
- Is its defrost performance suitable?
- Can the electrical installation support it?
- Is it maintainable and repairable?
- Is there enough evidence to support the important claims?

Do not force a single winner if the evidence is insufficient. Explain what information is still missing.

**Core principle:** First establish what technical solution is needed. Only then decide which compromises are acceptable within the available budget.

## 💡 Final thought

Choosing an air conditioner is not just buying a product. It means selecting a technical system that should work for many years, adapting to the building, the weather and the needs of the people living there.

Not everyone needs to buy the most expensive model. But everyone deserves to understand what they are buying, which compromises they are accepting and which claims are supported by actual evidence.

**Don't let marketing choose for you. Understand what you need first - then choose.**
