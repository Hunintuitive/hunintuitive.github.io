### TL;DR

Our attention is constantly being pulled in different directions - by technology, notifications, habits, and even our own thoughts. But we don't have to accept every default setting. By reducing unnecessary distractions, recognising when we get caught in unhelpful thought patterns, and making deliberate room for the things that genuinely matter to us, we can regain a little more control over how we spend our time and attention. The goal isn't to become perfectly productive. It's to live a little more intentionally - and enjoy the process along the way.

# Better Than the Default
### 🌍 Reclaiming our attention, reshaping our habits, and choosing how we want to live

We live in a world that constantly competes for our attention.

Notifications, messages, endless feeds, recommendations, breaking news, and an almost unlimited supply of things to watch or read. There is always something asking us to look, listen, react, or respond.

And because these things are so readily available, it is easy to forget that we don't actually have to give them our attention.

Recently, I listened to a conversation between Steven Bartlett and Dr. Sahar Yousef, a cognitive neuroscientist. It brought together several ideas I had encountered before, but one thought stayed with me:

**We deserve better than the default settings.**

{{< youtube yePLuwL3FbM >}}

I think this applies to much more than the way we use our phones. It is also about how we organise our time, develop our habits, deal with our thoughts, and decide what deserves a place in our lives.

## 💻 Are our tools really working for us?

Technology has given us extraordinary opportunities. We can communicate, collaborate, learn, and create in ways that would have been difficult to imagine not so long ago.

I genuinely appreciate these possibilities. I enjoy technology, and I am certainly not immune to its attractions.

But there is a question worth asking: what is a particular tool actually optimised for?

A product presented as a productivity tool may also be designed to keep us engaged. A notification can be useful, but it can also interrupt us. A platform may help us connect with others while simultaneously encouraging us to spend more time there than we intended.

These incentives are not necessarily the same as our own interests.

That doesn't mean every technology company has bad intentions, or that every digital tool is harmful. It simply means that we should not automatically assume that a product's default settings are designed around what is best for us.

Perhaps the first step is to ask a simple question:

*What does this tool want from me, and what do I actually need from it?*

## 🧠 Distraction doesn't always come from outside

We often think of distraction as something external: a notification, a message, someone interrupting us, or a phone lighting up on the table.

But sometimes the distraction begins inside us.

When a task becomes difficult, confusing, frustrating, or emotionally uncomfortable, we may instinctively look for something easier. Checking a message, opening another tab, or scrolling through a feed offers a quick change of focus.

It can feel like a small break, but sometimes it is simply an escape from the discomfort of concentrating.

Recognising this makes a difference. Instead of automatically blaming ourselves for lacking discipline, we can become curious about what is happening.

Am I genuinely taking a break, or am I avoiding something difficult? What am I feeling right now? Is there a smaller, more manageable step I could take?

We don't need to respond to every uncomfortable moment by reaching for a screen.

Sometimes the better choice is to stay with the task a little longer. At other times, a proper break is exactly what we need.

## 🔽 Reduce the noise before demanding more focus

One idea from the conversation that I particularly liked was the distinction between *signal* and *noise*.

The signal is what really matters: the task, conversation, or activity that deserves our attention.

The noise is everything competing with it.

We often try to improve our concentration by demanding more of ourselves. But perhaps a more effective first step is to reduce the unnecessary demands on our attention.

For example:

- Turn off notifications that do not require an immediate response.
- Decide which people genuinely need to be able to reach you at any time.
- Use priority or VIP notifications for important contacts, while allowing less urgent messages to wait.
- Check email and messages at chosen times instead of reacting to every alert.
- Put your phone out of reach when you need to concentrate.

This isn't about becoming unavailable. It is about being reachable without being constantly interrupted.

And focus is not only about choosing one thing over another. Sometimes it means saying no to something genuinely attractive because accepting it would take time and attention away from a more important commitment.

That can be surprisingly difficult. We may have to decline a good opportunity to protect something we consider even more valuable.

## 🚶 Sometimes we need to step away

There is another interesting aspect of how we think: trying harder is not always the best way to solve a problem.

When we get stuck, we can spend a long time going over the same thoughts without making progress. Sometimes changing our surroundings or doing something completely different can help.

Going for a walk, washing the dishes, taking a shower, or simply stepping outside can give our minds a different context in which to work.

This doesn't mean that every problem will suddenly solve itself. But a change of activity can help us loosen our grip on one line of thought and notice connections we might otherwise miss.

I find this particularly valuable because we often treat every quiet moment as an opportunity to consume more information.

Perhaps some moments should remain quiet.

Not every pause needs a podcast, a video, or another scroll through a feed. We can allow ourselves time to think, observe, and let ideas develop without immediately filling the space.

## 💭 When thinking becomes a loop

The conversation also raised a more sensitive subject: negative thinking and rumination.

There is an important difference between acknowledging a painful experience, trying to understand it, and becoming trapped in the same distressing thoughts over and over again.

Talking about trauma, seeking support, and revisiting difficult experiences can be important parts of recovery. People process painful events differently, and there is no universal timetable or simple formula for recovery.

So I would not interpret this as advice to suppress difficult feelings or simply think positively.

But it may be worth asking ourselves a gentle question from time to time:

*Is this reflection helping me understand something, or am I caught in a loop that leaves me feeling just as stuck as before?*

If we notice that we are repeatedly returning to the same thoughts without gaining anything from it, perhaps we can try changing what we do next.

That might mean going for a walk, engaging in a practical activity, talking to someone we trust, seeking professional support, or taking one small step towards something that matters to us.

We don't have to deny what happened to make room for something else in our lives.

Our painful experiences may be part of our story, but they do not have to become the whole story.

## 🎧 What do we want to make room for?

![Alt text](/en/post/betterthandefault/me.png)

It is easy to frame this entire subject around what we should stop doing.

Less scrolling. Fewer notifications. Less unnecessary screen time. Less reacting to everything.

But I think there is a more interesting question:

**What would we like to have more room for?**

More meaningful conversations? More time with people we care about? Reading, learning, creating, moving, exploring, or simply enjoying a moment without feeling the need to document or share it?

And let's not forget the simple pleasures that make life enjoyable. Listening to music, getting lost in a favourite song, or taking a proper break. And by a proper break, I don't necessarily mean sitting quietly and doing nothing. Sometimes it means turning up the music and letting yourself move to a rhythm you love. In my case, that could even turn into a rather enthusiastic, spontaneous breakdancing session. 
🕺😄

{{< youtube mAfYbykA2aE >}}

Why not? Not every moment needs to be productive. Some moments are simply worth enjoying.

If we only focus on removing distractions, we may end up with empty space that we quickly fill again.

Instead, we can deliberately give that space to things that reflect our values, interests, and what makes us feel good about being alive.

For me, this is an ongoing experiment rather than something I have mastered.

I still spend a lot of time at a computer, and I am certainly not immune to the attraction of quick rewards: another video, another interesting link, another distraction.

But I have been trying to limit my smartphone scrolling and use my computer more deliberately for much of my digital life. I find that sitting down at a computer makes it easier for me to choose what I want to do, rather than carrying a device that constantly presents new distractions.

Of course, a computer can be just as distracting. The point is not that one device is inherently better than another. It is that the way we use our tools, and the environment we create around them, can make a difference.

Small changes can help: choosing when to check messages, keeping the phone away during focused activities, making time for offline experiences, listening to music simply because we enjoy it, or deliberately saying no to something that would otherwise consume our attention.

We don't have to change everything at once.

## 🌱 A little more choice

![Alt text](/en/post/betterthandefault/choice.png)

I don't think the goal should be to control every minute of our lives or to become perfectly disciplined.

Technology is not going away, and neither are our human tendencies to seek comfort, novelty, and easy rewards.

But we can become more aware of the forces shaping our habits. We can question defaults instead of accepting them automatically. We can create environments that make our priorities easier to follow.

And perhaps most importantly, we can measure progress not only by how much distraction we eliminate, but by what we make room for instead.

The question I want to leave with is this:

*If I keep giving my attention to the things that ask for it most insistently, will I still have enough left for the things that matter most to me?*

We deserve better than the default settings.

Not because we can always resist every distraction, but because our attention, our time, and our lives are worth choosing more deliberately.
